_Almost every Forth in this portfolio is a descendant of one 15,000-line pile of
`.fs` files written by someone else. This is the article where I actually read it
properly instead of porting it, and say what makes it good._

## TL;DR

- **What it is:** [WF32](https://github.com/alextangent/wf32) — Alex McDonald's
  32-bit Windows Forth, Forth-2012 conformant with minor omissions, BSD-2-Clause,
  version 0.06.08. Roughly **15,500 lines of Forth**, of which the kernel is 4,639
  and the meta-compiler 1,253.
- **It has no C.** There is no host compiler anywhere in the build. WF32 is
  **meta-compiled**: a running WF32 reads the kernel source, assembles x86 with an
  assembler *also written in Forth*, lays out three separate dictionaries in
  malloc'd buffers, resolves forward references, and writes a **Windows PE
  executable** — which imports exactly two functions from `kernel32.dll`.
- **Subroutine threading, not indirect threading.** A colon definition is raw
  machine code. `dup` compiles to a `call rel32`; the return stack *is* `esp`, so
  `;` is a `ret`.
- **Then it stops emitting calls.** Every header carries a **`comp` field** — a
  per-word code generator — plus the word's **byte length** (`ofa`) and its
  **stack effect** (`ste`). `compile,` is one line: `dup >comp perform`. Words
  compile themselves, and about **half the primitives compile themselves as
  inlined bytes** instead of a call.
- **The measurement, from the author's own comment** in `optinline.fs`: inlining
  costs around 10% more code space and buys "a 200% improvement in performance".
- **Where it goes:** WF32 → [WF64](/posts/wf64) → [WF65](/posts/wf65) →
  [WF66](/posts/wf66) → [MF66](/posts/mf66) → [MF67](/posts/mf67). See the
  [timeline](/timeline).

## Before all this: a hex editor and several weeks

I fell for Forth when I was young, and I fell for it for the reason everyone who
falls for it does: it is the only language where you can hold the entire
implementation in your head and still do real work with it.

What I actually did with that affection was port **FIG-Forth** to my own
machines. Not with a cross-assembler — I did not have one. I had the FIG model
listing, a machine, and a **hex editor**. You work through the primitives one at
a time, hand-assembling each one, writing the bytes in, fixing the link fields by
hand, and counting offsets on paper. It took weeks per machine. The reward is
that at some point the thing wakes up: you type a word and it answers, and from
then on the system extends *itself* and you never touch hex again. You have
bootstrapped out of the tar pit on a handful of hand-assembled primitives and a
lot of patience.

That experience is the whole reason I have spent a season writing compilers. It
teaches a specific thing that nothing else teaches as cheaply: **the boundary
between "the machine" and "the language" is a decision you get to make, and it is
much lower down than you think.** Most languages hide that boundary. Forth hands
you the trowel.

## Then: Win32Forth, and what an open environment feels like

The next chapter for me was **Win32Forth** — Win32For, as everyone typed it — on
Windows. It was developed by **Tom Zimmer** and **Andrew McKewan**, with
contributions from a long list of people around them, and released into the
public domain. It has to be one of the most generous pieces of software of its
era.

What made it matter was not the language, it was the *environment*. Win32Forth
was complete and it was **open all the way down**. It had an editor, a browser, a
debugger, a decompiler, an assembler, a full Win32 binding, COM support, a class
library, and the ability to save your session out as a standalone `.exe` — and
every one of those was written in Forth, in source, in the box, editable by you,
at runtime. You could type a word and see its machine code. You could redefine
something in the middle of the system and keep going. Nothing was a black box
that you filed a bug against; if it annoyed you, you fixed it at 11pm and carried
on.

That is a quality modern toolchains have very little of, and it is the quality I
have been chasing across this entire portfolio — the reason the compilers here
are [source-in, not image-based](/posts/not-image-based), and why they keep
[text at every stage](/posts/text-at-every-stage).

Win32Forth is also, directly, WF32's ancestor. The `LICENSE` file says so in
plain terms: the original Win32Forth was public domain, its basic structure —
"for instance, meta compilation" — and many of its capabilities are employed in
WF32, while the kernel, the assembler and much of the supporting code have been
completely rewritten, with the original contributions of Zimmer and McKewan
acknowledged. That is exactly the right way to inherit from something.

## What WF32 is

**WF32 is Alex McDonald's** (`alextangent`), and it is a serious piece of work.
The kernel headers describe it as "a fully optimising, STC based, ANS Forth
compliant kernel", and its copyright line spans 2005 to 2017. It is BSD-2-Clause.
I adopted it as the seed of this portfolio's entire Forth line, and everything
from [WF64](/posts/wf64) onward is descended from its primitives and its compiler
model.

Three things make it worth a careful read, and they are what the rest of this
article is about: **it builds itself**, **it compiles to real machine code**, and
**it inlines that code eagerly enough to change the performance class of the
language**.

## Part 1 — meta-compilation: a Forth that writes a Windows .exe

The build has no C compiler, no linker, and no external assembler. It has a
running WF32 and some source files. That is the entire toolchain.

Running `gMeta32.fs` on a live WF32 sets a handful of switches and points at the
sources:

```forth
create kern-name ," gkernel32.exe"
create kern-src  ," src\kernel\gkernel32.fs"
create kern-cmp  ," src\kernel\gmeta-compiler32.fs"

true  value [meta]                      \ meta compiler flag
true  value [tail-call]                 \ generate tail-calls
$400000      to image-origin            \ where target image will run
```

Then it loads the meta-compiler and compiles the kernel *through* it. Four
mechanisms carry the whole thing.

### Three dictionaries, built as offsets into host memory

The target image is not one blob. WF32 splits it into **code**, **app** (data)
and **sys** (headers), each with its own dictionary pointer, each built into a
separate `malloc`'d host buffer:

```forth
$10000 dup malloc to image-codeptr   \ where code is built
$10000 dup malloc to image-appptr    \ where target data is built
$10000 dup malloc to image-sysptr    \ where target heads are built

image-origin image-csep + image-codeptr - constant tcode-base
```

That `tcode-base` constant is the trick that makes cross-compilation readable.
Every target address is a host address plus a fixed bias, so `tcode-here` reports
where a word will live *when the image runs at `$400000`*, not where it currently
sits in the host's heap. Relative call displacements therefore compute correctly
during the build, before the image has ever been loaded. There is a full parallel
vocabulary of `t-!`, `t-,`, `tcode-c,`, `tsys-w!` and friends: the ordinary Forth
store-and-comma words, redirected at the target.

Separating headers from code from data also means a finished application can drop
`.sys` entirely and ship without a dictionary — the classic Forth turnkey — and
it is why the executable comes out with sections literally named `.code`, `.app`
and `.sys`.

### Four wordlists, and words that compile themselves

Meta-compilation's real difficulty is that the word `dup` now means two different
things: the host's `dup`, which the compiler itself needs in order to run, and
the target's `dup`, which is a thing being *built*. WF32 resolves this with the
search order:

```forth
: in-primitive  only forward also target also primitive ;
```

- **`target`** — words already built in the image.
- **`primitive`** — meta-compiler words defined with `p:`, which *generate* code
  (`literal`, `postpone`, `[']`, `to`, `is`).
- **`forward`** — words not defined yet.

Every target word is created with a `does>` of exactly one line:

```forth
does> dup tgt.compile @ execute ;      \ what target words do
```

So during meta-compilation, **mentioning a word compiles it**. The default
`tgt.compile` is `target-call,`, which assembles a `call` to the word's target
xt. This is the same "the word knows how to compile itself" idea that the runtime
kernel uses — and it is the hinge that Part 3 turns on, because changing that one
field per word is how inlining gets switched on.

### Forward references, and placeholder compile tokens

A kernel is a cyclic graph; something always gets used before it is defined. WF32
handles this two ways at once.

For *calls*, an unknown name auto-creates a forward record: the call is assembled
with a dummy displacement, and the location to patch is chained onto that
record's resolve list. At the end, `fwd-fixup` walks every forward, looks the name
up in `target`, and back-patches each site. It reports its own score —
`n resolved, m unresolved` — checks the stack is clean, aborts the build if
anything is left over, and (a detail I enjoy more than I should) plays the
Windows system-hand sound at you if it isn't.

For *header fields*, there is a neater problem: every header wants to hold the
target xt of `compile,`, `xt-call,` or `inline,` in its compile-token fields, but
those words don't exist yet when the first header is built. So the meta-compiler
stamps negative sentinels:

```forth
-2 constant ct-compile,
-3 constant ct-call,
-4 constant ct-inline,
```

...chains every site on `ct-link`, and later `ct-fixup` walks the chain replacing
each sentinel with the real target xt. It is a two-pass linker written in about
forty lines of Forth.

### `: : code ] ;` — and it must be last

My favourite line in the codebase is the final definition in the meta-compiler,
with its own comment:

```forth
: :             code ]             ; \ standard colon def MUST BE LAST
```

The meta-compiler ends by *redefining colon itself* on the host, so that from
that instant `:` means "open a target definition and enter the meta-compiling
loop". It must be last because after that point the host can no longer define
host words — the compiler has burned the ladder behind it. Above it in the same
block, `,` `!` `here` `allot` `align` `'` `>body` are all redirected to the
target too. Six lines of aliasing and the language has changed meaning
underneath you.

The loop those definitions feed is small enough to quote whole:

```forth
: ]     ( -- )
        meta-state on
        in-primitive
        begin   token find
                if      execute                  \ the word compiles itself
                else    count meta-number?
                        if      [primitive] literal
                        else    meta-not-found   \ → forward reference
                        then
                then
                meta-state @ 0=
        until   ;
```

Find it and execute it; if it's a number, compile a literal; otherwise create a
forward reference. That is the entire cross-compiler driver.

### The PE writer is a Forth DSL

Finally, `imageman.fs` emits the executable, and the PE/COFF format is expressed
as a little declarative language:

```forth
: std-img2exe ( addr len -- )
      BUILD
        EXETYPE          SUBSYSTEM
        STD-EXELOAD      LOADPOINT
        IMAGE-ENTRY      ENTRYPOINT
        S" .code"                  SECTION
        STD-CODE                     SECTIONTYPE
        IMAGE-CODEPTR IMAGE-CACTUAL  SECTIONDATA
                                   ENDSECTION
        ...
        S" .idata"                 SECTION
        S" kernel32.dll"               IMPORT-DLL
          0 S" GetProcAddress"           IMPORT-FUNC
          0 S" LoadLibraryA"             IMPORT-FUNC
                                       ENDIMPORTS
      endbuild ;
```

Look at the import table. **Two functions.** `LoadLibraryA` and
`GetProcAddress`. Everything else Win32Forth-style Forth does on Windows — the
console, files, exceptions, GDI, the lot — is bound at runtime by the Forth
itself, from those two hooks. The resulting `.exe` has essentially no static
dependency surface.

## Part 2 — subroutine threading, and the header that makes it interesting

WF32 is **STC**: subroutine-threaded code. There is no inner interpreter, no
`NEXT`, no threaded list of execution tokens. A colon definition is a run of
`call` instructions terminated by `ret`, and the machine's own call/return
mechanism is the interpreter.

The register assignment is the one this whole family still uses (WF64 carries it
up to 64-bit almost unchanged):

| register | role |
|---|---|
| `eax` | top of data stack, in a register |
| `ebp` | data stack pointer (grows downward) |
| `esp` | **return stack** — the hardware stack |
| `ebx` | user-area pointer |
| `ecx`, `edx` | scratch; `ecx` also carries the body address for `does>` |

Keeping TOS in `eax` is what makes the primitives tiny, and putting the return
stack on `esp` is what makes STC free: `>r` is `push eax`, `r>` is `pop eax`, and
`;` is one byte of `ret`.

Primitives are written in the Forth assembler, in the kernel source, looking like
this:

```forth
code dup        ( n -- n n )    \ duplicate top entry on data stack
    1 2 in/out
      mov     { -cell ebp } eax
      next; inline

code @          ( addr -- n )
      mov     eax { eax }
      next; inline
```

`next;` is a macro that closes the assembler, emits any stack-pointer
adjustment implied by `in/out`, records the word's length, and emits `ret`. So
`dup` — declared `1 2 in/out`, one cell in, two out — gets an automatic `sub
ebp, 4` before its return. Six bytes of code:

```
mov  dword [ebp-4], eax     ; 89 45 FC
sub  ebp, 4                 ; 83 ED 04
ret                         ; C3
```

Now the part that matters. Each header is not just a link, a name and an xt —
here is `gstructs.fs`'s `head%`, with its padding arithmetic trimmed and the
comments condensed from the source's own:

```forth
begin-structure head%
  lfield:  head.link   \ link field
  lfield:  head.ct     \ semantics: ' compile,  (or ' execute if IMMEDIATE)
  lfield:  head.xtptr  \ pointer to the xt
  lfield:  head.comp   \ HOW TO COMPILE ME — default ' xt-call,
  lfield:  head.rec    \ recognizer
  wfield:  head.vfa    \ view field: source line number
  wfield:  head.ofa    \ length of the xt's code, used for inlining
  wfield:  head.stk    \ ste: two bytes (in, out); -ve = unknown
  bfield:  head.tfa    \ type flag
  0 +field head.nt     \ counted name string — the name token
end-structure
```

Four of those fields are the article. `head.comp` is **a code generator per
word**. `head.ofa` is **how many bytes that word's body occupies**. `head.stk` is
**its arity**. `head.vfa` is the source line, which is why the decompiler can
tell you where a word came from.

And with `head.comp` in place, the compiler's core is a single line:

```forth
: compile,       ( xt -- )          dup >comp perform ;
```

That is the whole design. `compile,` doesn't know how to compile anything. It
asks the word. The default answer is `xt-call,`, which emits `E8 rel32`. But
*any* word can answer differently, and the rest of the system is built out of
words answering differently:

- **`immediate`** sets `head.ct` to `execute` — the standard behaviour, expressed
  in the same mechanism rather than as a flag.
- **`compiles>`** lets a definition carry custom compilation semantics inline;
  that's how `if`, `do`, `case` and `abort"` are written.
- **`compiles-for`** installs an optimiser as some other word's generator, from
  outside that word — which is how a separately-loaded file can retrofit
  optimisation onto the kernel without touching it.
- **`inline`** sets it to `inline,`.

This is the "compilation token" design for stateless compilation, and the source
cites its origin directly — the header-ideas note at `complang.tuwien.ac.at`. It
costs three cells per header and it buys a compiler with no central `case`
statement in it anywhere.

## Part 3 — eager inlining, and why it is nearly free

### The mechanism

Two definitions, five lines, and this is the entire inliner:

```forth
: inline, ( xt -- ) \ inline a word
    dup >name head.ofa w@ copy-code ; \ get the length, inline it

: inline ( -- ) \ code will be inlined
    tail-call 0= if                   \ if no calls, inlineable
      ['] inline, compiles-me         \ set my own comp field
    then ;
```

`inline,` is a `memcpy`. It looks up the word's recorded byte length and copies
that many bytes of its body straight into the definition being compiled. No IR,
no re-encoding, no register allocation — the primitive was already written as a
position-independent fragment, so the fragment simply *is* the inline expansion.

The detail that makes this work cleanly is where the length stops. When a colon
definition closes, the length is computed as:

```forth
exit&[ 1+ (ofa-calc)     \ accounts for RET
```

— from the xt **plus one**, so `ofa` deliberately **excludes the trailing `ret`
byte**. Same for `code` words: the `next` macro calls `ofa-calc` *before* it
assembles the `ret`. So `ofa` is always "the body without the return", which is
exactly the byte range you want to splice in. `dup`'s recorded length is 6, not
7 — `SEE DUP` reports `len=6`, disassembles six bytes, and then has to go one
instruction further on purpose to show you the `ret`.

### The guard, which is also the tail-call optimiser

Copying machine code is only safe if it contains no PC-relative reference to
anywhere outside itself — and in x86 a `call rel32` is exactly that. Copy a word
containing a call to a new address and the call now lands somewhere arbitrary.

WF32 detects this with a variable that is already there for another reason:

```forth
: xt-call,  ( xt -- )
    $e8 xt-rel,                    \ compile call to xt
    code-here to tail-call ;       \ possible tail call
```

Every emitted call records the address just past itself in `tail-call`. `:` zeros
it on entry. Two different optimisations read it:

**Tail calls.** At `exit`, if `code-here` is still equal to `tail-call`, the last
thing emitted was a call, so the `call X; ret` pair is rewritten in place:

```forth
: exit ( -- )
    sync-code
    code-here tail-call = if
      tail-call 5 - call>jmp       \ E8 → E9: turn the call into a jump
    then
    postpone unnest ;              \ still emit the ret; may be a branch target
```

One byte changes — `E8` becomes `E9` — and a call/return pair becomes a jump. The
`ret` is still emitted afterwards, deliberately, because the location may be the
target of a forward branch from an `if`.

**Inlining.** At `; inline`, `tail-call` being **zero** proves no call was ever
emitted in this definition — which is precisely the "safe to copy" condition. One
variable, two optimisations, no extra bookkeeping.

There is a nice asymmetry here: the meta-compiler's `inline` has no such guard.
During the kernel build the author is assumed to know what they're doing (and the
`next` macro's comment says so outright — the programmer needs to be smart about
inline). The guard exists in the runtime kernel, for *your* code. If you write
`: foo ... ; inline` and the body contains a call, `inline` quietly declines and
you get a normal callable word. It fails safe.

### How eager is "eager"?

Counting the kernel: **201 `code` primitives, 108 of them marked `inline`** —
just over half. And the split is exactly where you'd draw it by hand:

| inlined | left as calls |
|---|---|
| `dup swap drop over nip` | `move cmove fill` |
| `+ - and or xor invert negate` | `* / mod /mod */ */mod` |
| `@ ! c@ c! +!` | `um* um/mod m* sm/rem fm/mod` |
| `>r r> i j unloop` | `d+ d- d< dnegate` (all double-cell) |
| `bra ?bra` (branch templates) | `depth within 2swap 2over` |

Small, hot, register-shuffling words inline. Big multi-instruction routines and
anything with an internal loop stay callable. That's the space/speed trade made
one word at a time, by someone who knew which words were hot.

And for the smallest words, inlining is *smaller as well as faster*. Calling `@`
costs 5 bytes at the call site (`E8` + rel32), plus a taken call and a matching
return at runtime. Inlined, `@` is **two bytes** — `8B 00` — and no branches at
all. `dup` costs 6 bytes
inline against 5 for the call: one byte worse, one call/ret pair better. That is
where the author's own number comes from, in the header comment of
`optinline.fs`: inlining costs around 10% more code space and shows "a 200%
improvement in performance".

Note also what the branch primitives are doing in that table. `bra` and `?bra`
are marked `inline` and their bodies are `jmp 0` / `jz 0` with a placeholder
displacement. They are not really callable words at all — they are
**instruction templates**, and `if`/`while`/`until` "inline" them and then patch
the displacement with `>resolve`. The inliner doubles as the code emitter for
control flow.

## Part 4 — the optimiser layer, retrofitted from outside

Everything so far is in the kernel. Two more files, loaded *afterwards* at
extension time, then reach in and change how the kernel's own words compile —
without recompiling the kernel. This is `compiles-for` earning its keep.

### Deferred literals

`optliterals32.fs` starts by making `literal` stop emitting code:

```forth
: litstack ( n xt -- ) drop lits spush ;  \ stack literal
' litstack compiles-for literal
' litsync  is sync-code
```

Literals now accumulate on a compile-time stack. `sync-code` — a `defer` the
kernel calls at every point where pending code must be flushed — becomes
`litsync`, which materialises whatever literals are still outstanding. Between
those two points, the compiler is holding a small window of known constants.

### Folding them into addressing modes

With that window, memory operations rewrite themselves:

```forth
: opt@  ( xt -- ) lits>0? if litstart push-tos var->tos  else inline, then ;
' opt@ compiles-for @
```

`myvar @` no longer emits `mov eax, imm32` then `mov eax, [eax]` — it emits a
single `mov eax, [myvar]`. `!` with two pending literals collapses to a
`mov dword [addr], imm32` with no register traffic at all. `+!` folds to
`add [addr], imm32`. And every one of those paths ends `else inline, then` — if
there's no constant to fold, fall back to splicing the primitive.

### Constant folding by *running the word*

The best piece is `nseopt`, the "no side effect" optimiser, which is where
`head.stk` finally pays off:

```forth
: nseopt   ( xt -- )
    dup >name (in/out@)           \ get the word's declared arity
    over lits sdepth <= if        \ enough pending literals to do it now?
      ... pop that many literals, EXECUTE the word, push its results back ...
      exit
    then 2drop
    lits>0? if  ... single-literal peephole from opt-lit-table1 ...  then
    inline, ;                     \ else just inline it
```

Read the middle branch again: if the word declares it takes two inputs and there
are two constants pending, the compiler **executes the target word at compile
time** and replaces it with its results. `2 3 *` becomes the literal `6` — not
via a special case for `*`, but because `*` said `2 1 in/out` in its header and
the compiler took it at its word. Arity metadata written for stack-pointer
adjustment turns out to be exactly what constant folding needs.

Failing that, a table of 28 entries handles one-literal cases, and it is
genuinely peephole-quality:

```forth
create opt-lit-table1
  ' +      , ' add-immed  ,
  ' *      , ' imul-immed ,
  ' =      , ' opt=       ,
  ' dup    , ' push-tos   ,
  ...
```

`imul-immed` is the fun one — multiply by `-1` becomes `negate`, by `0` becomes a
move, by `1` disappears entirely, by `2` becomes `2*`, and by any power of two
becomes a shift. Otherwise, a three-operand `imul`.

And note the fallthrough at the bottom of `nseopt`: `inline,`. Installing the
optimiser on `*` — a word the kernel did *not* mark `inline` — means that in the
no-constant case `*` now gets inlined anyway. The optimiser layer is quietly more
aggressive than the kernel's own annotations.

## What it feels like to use

All of that machinery is visible from the REPL, which is the Win32Forth
inheritance showing through. `SEE` decompiles from the header fields directly —
this example is the one the assembler's own documentation uses (`asm/readme.txt`,
§6):

```
see dup
code dup ( 1 -- 2 )
\ dup is defined in src/kernel/gkernel32.fs at line 124
\ ' nseopt compiled
\ code=$401135 len=6 type=129
( $0 )    mov     dword { $-4 ebp } eax             \ 8945FC
( $3 )    sub     ebp $4                            \ 83ED04
( $6 )    ret                                       \ C3 ( end )
```

Every line of that is a header field being read back. `( 1 -- 2 )` is `head.stk`.
The source file and line are `head.vfa`. `' nseopt compiled` is `head.comp` —
it's telling you which code generator this word will use, and that the optimiser
has claimed it. `len=6` is `head.ofa`, the inline length. And the disassembly
runs for exactly `head.ofa` bytes — the decompiler then peeks one instruction
past the end and prints the `ret` separately, precisely because `ofa` excludes
it.

That is the open environment I described at the top, in one command: the language
will tell you its own machine code, its own arity, its own compilation strategy,
and where it was defined. There is an assembler in the image, a disassembler in
the image, `FSAVE` to write your session back out as a fresh `.exe`, and a
`CODE ... END-CODE` escape hatch to define a new primitive in x86 without leaving
the REPL. Nothing is behind glass.

## What I took from it

I ported the primitives and the STC compiler up to 64-bit as
[WF64](/posts/wf64), and every Forth since has been a descendant. The pieces that
survived every rewrite are the ones described above:

- **STC with TOS in a register and the return stack on the hardware stack.**
  Unchanged from WF32 to [MF67](/posts/mf67), only widened — `eax`→`rax`,
  `ebp`→`rbp`, and on Apple Silicon the arm64 equivalent.
- **A per-word code generator.** WF32's `head.comp` is the direct ancestor of
  [WF66](/posts/wf66)'s token IR: both answer "how do I compile this word?" per
  word rather than in one central switch. WF66 just made the answer a data
  structure it could optimise before emitting, instead of a routine that emits
  immediately.
- **Eager inlining as the default, not a pass.** [WF65](/posts/wf65)'s
  rewind-and-replay peephole and MF66's tokenizing optimiser are both attempts to
  keep WF32's "inline first, ask questions never" performance while gaining the
  ability to look back at what was emitted.
- **Recorded arity as an optimisation input.** `in/out` becomes stack-effect
  tracking in every later Forth here.

The one thing I did *not* keep is meta-compilation, and it is worth being honest
about why. WF32's build is elegant and self-sufficient — but it requires a
working WF32 to build a WF32, which is a chicken-and-egg problem every time you
change the header layout, and it makes the compiler unavailable as a component to
anything that isn't Forth. From WF64 onward the Forth is a Rust program that
brings up a Forth, which loses the beautiful closure of the loop and gains a
toolchain, a test harness, and an assembler ([JASM](/posts/jasm), later
[MRASM](/posts/mrasm)) that the other languages in this portfolio could then
share. That is the trade the whole [portfolio](/timeline) is built on.

WF32 is still the more impressive artefact. Fifteen thousand lines of Forth, no
other language anywhere in the build, and out the other end comes a Windows
executable with two imports and an optimising compiler inside it.
